\chapter*{Executive Summary}\label{executive-summary}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Executive Summary}

The reproducibility crisis in academic and applied research threatens
the credibility of sustainability innovations in supply chain
management. This thesis investigates the extent to which open-source
computational tools---specifically RStudio and Jupyter Notebooks---can
enable reproducible research practices within the Value Chain Hackers
(VCH) Lab at Windesheim University of Applied Sciences.

Through a comprehensive case study approach, this research empirically
tests the reproducibility of supply chain sustainability interventions,
identifies systemic barriers across technical, organizational, and
contextual domains, and proposes a structured reproducible research
framework aligned with the Enterprise Data Reference Model (EDRM) and
FAIR principles.

The findings reveal that while RStudio and Jupyter Notebooks offer
powerful capabilities for transparent and repeatable workflows, their
effectiveness is severely constrained by institutional factors including
inadequate documentation standards, absence of version control
practices, ephemeral data storage, and limited methodological training.
Analysis of five VCH Lab research tracks (Bakery, CRM, Organizational,
Textile, and Cacao) demonstrates that reproducibility failures are
systemic rather than individual, stemming from educational models that
prioritize creative outputs over process transparency.

This thesis contributes both theoretical insights into reproducibility
challenges in business innovation contexts and practical deliverables
including a working prototype infrastructure integrating JupyterHub,
Quarto, Git/GitHub, and n8n automation. The proposed framework provides
Windesheim with an actionable pathway to transform applied research
processes into verifiable, cumulative knowledge systems capable of
supporting evidence-based sustainability innovations.

\textbf{Keywords:} reproducible research, computational notebooks,
supply chain sustainability, Value Chain Hackers, RStudio, Jupyter, FAIR
principles, applied research methodology

\chapter{Chapter 1: Introduction}\label{chapter-1-introduction}

\section{1.1 Background and Context}\label{background-and-context}

The credibility of research---both academic and applied---depends
fundamentally on its reproducibility. Reproducible research ensures that
analyses, findings, and conclusions can be independently verified,
validated, and built upon by other researchers (\textbf{baker2016?}).
When research cannot be reproduced, trust erodes, resources are wasted,
and decision-making becomes compromised (\textbf{goodman2016?}). This
crisis of confidence has become particularly acute in computational
research, where even studies claiming to use ``open data'' frequently
fail independent reproduction attempts (\textbf{stodden2018?};
\textbf{collberg2016?}).

In the domain of supply chain sustainability, reproducibility takes on
additional urgency. Organizations, policymakers, and consumers
increasingly demand verifiable evidence that sustainability
interventions deliver genuine environmental and social impacts
(\textbf{montabon2016?}). Without reproducible research practices,
businesses risk implementing ineffective or misleading practices,
regulators lack reliable evidence for policy development, and the
broader sustainability movement loses credibility
(\textbf{ioannidis2005?}).

The Value Chain Hackers (VCH) Lab at Windesheim University of Applied
Sciences exemplifies both the opportunities and challenges of applied
sustainability research. Established to ``revolutionize the supply chain
industry through relentless innovation, spirited collaboration, and
hands-on practical application,'' the VCH Lab brings together students,
academic researchers, and industry partners to tackle real-world supply
chain challenges. However, preliminary investigation of past VCH Lab
projects reveals systematic reproducibility failures: missing
documentation, untraceable analytical processes, ephemeral data storage,
and absence of version control practices.

These failures are not unique to VCH Lab. They reflect broader
institutional patterns in applied research environments where creative
problem-solving is prioritized over methodological rigor, where
educational models reward outputs rather than transparent processes, and
where technical infrastructure for reproducible workflows remains
underdeveloped (\textbf{freire2012?}).

\section{1.2 The Reproducibility Crisis: From Science to
Business}\label{the-reproducibility-crisis-from-science-to-business}

The reproducibility crisis emerged into public consciousness through
high-profile failures in biomedical research, psychology, and
computational science (\textbf{baker2016?}). A 2016 survey of 1,500
scientists found that more than 70\% had failed to reproduce another
researcher's work, and more than 50\% had failed to reproduce their own
results. While initially framed as a crisis of scientific integrity,
reproducibility challenges extend far beyond academic laboratories.

In business innovation and applied research contexts, irreproducible
findings carry direct economic and social consequences:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Resource waste}: Organizations invest in interventions based
  on research that cannot be validated
\item
  \textbf{Regulatory uncertainty}: Policymakers lack reliable evidence
  for informed decision-making
\item
  \textbf{Stakeholder distrust}: Industry partners, funders, and
  communities lose confidence in research outputs
\item
  \textbf{Knowledge fragmentation}: Insights cannot accumulate across
  projects or teams
\end{enumerate}

Supply chain sustainability research faces particular reproducibility
challenges due to:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Data confidentiality}: Business-sensitive information limits
  data sharing (\textbf{freire2012?})
\item
  \textbf{Contextual complexity}: Supply chain interventions depend on
  specific organizational, geographic, and temporal contexts
\item
  \textbf{Multi-stakeholder dynamics}: Research involves diverse actors
  with competing interests
\item
  \textbf{Methodological pluralism}: Applied research integrates
  qualitative and quantitative methods in iterative cycles
\end{itemize}

\section{1.3 The Promise of Computational
Notebooks}\label{the-promise-of-computational-notebooks}

Open-source computational notebooks---particularly RStudio and Jupyter
Notebooks---have emerged as potential solutions to reproducibility
challenges. These tools enable researchers to create ``literate
programming'' documents that interweave narrative text, executable code,
data visualizations, and analytical outputs in a single, shareable
format (\textbf{rule2019?}).

The theoretical advantages are compelling:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Transparency}: All analytical steps are visible and documented
\item
  \textbf{Executability}: Code can be re-run to verify results
\item
  \textbf{Shareability}: Notebooks can be distributed via repositories
  like GitHub
\item
  \textbf{Integration}: Notebooks support multiple programming languages
  and data formats
\item
  \textbf{Accessibility}: Open-source tools reduce barriers to entry
\end{itemize}

However, the mere availability of these tools does not guarantee
reproducible research. As (\textbf{sandve2013?}) observe, ``the ability
to perform computational research reproducibly is independent from the
actual performance of computational research reproducibly.'' Tool
adoption requires complementary changes in institutional practices,
educational training, workflow design, and quality assurance mechanisms.

\section{1.4 The Value Chain Hackers Lab as Research
Context}\label{the-value-chain-hackers-lab-as-research-context}

The VCH Lab provides an ideal context for investigating reproducibility
in applied business innovation research. Characteristics include:

\textbf{Organizational structure}: The lab operates at the intersection
of academia and industry, with projects commissioned by external
partners addressing genuine sustainability challenges in sectors
including agriculture, textiles, electronics, and food systems.

\textbf{Student-led research}: Projects are primarily executed by
final-year students in 4-6 month cycles, creating natural constraints
around knowledge transfer, documentation practices, and methodological
consistency.

\textbf{Innovation focus}: The lab's mission emphasizes creative
problem-solving and rapid prototyping, potentially creating tension with
slower, more rigorous research documentation practices.

\textbf{Multi-track design}: Parallel research tracks (Bakery, CRM,
Organizational, Textile, Cacao) provide opportunities for comparative
analysis of reproducibility challenges across different domains.

\textbf{Existing infrastructure}: The lab has access to JupyterHub,
Git/GitHub, Quarto publishing tools, and workflow automation platforms
(n8n), providing technical foundations for reproducible research.

Preliminary investigation of five completed VCH Lab tracks reveals
systematic reproducibility failures:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Bakery track}: Single audio recording without transcript,
  metadata, or analytical documentation
\item
  \textbf{CRM track}: Conceptual innovation lacking methodological
  transparency or version control
\item
  \textbf{Organizational track}: Pedagogical model prioritizing
  creativity without explicit reproducibility requirements
\item
  \textbf{Textile track}: Complete data volatility due to dependence on
  ephemeral cloud tools (Canva, Miro)
\item
  \textbf{Cacao track}: Polished outputs lacking documented source
  selection, analytic synthesis, or validation processes
\end{itemize}

These patterns suggest that reproducibility failures are systemic rather
than individual, requiring institutional-level interventions rather than
student-focused remediation.

\section{1.5 Research Gap and
Contribution}\label{research-gap-and-contribution}

While extensive literature addresses reproducibility in academic
computational science (\textbf{stodden2018?}; \textbf{collberg2016?};
\textbf{sandve2013?}), limited research examines reproducibility
challenges in applied business innovation contexts, particularly in
sustainability-focused labs operating at the academia-industry
interface.

This thesis addresses three interconnected gaps:

\textbf{Empirical gap}: Most reproducibility studies focus on academic
publications rather than applied research conducted in educational
innovation labs. This research provides systematic documentation of
reproducibility barriers across multiple real-world supply chain
sustainability projects.

\textbf{Methodological gap}: Existing reproducibility frameworks (FAIR
principles, Open Science guidelines) are designed primarily for academic
research. This thesis adapts and extends these frameworks for business
innovation contexts where data confidentiality, contextual specificity,
and stakeholder sensitivities constrain full transparency.

\textbf{Practical gap}: Limited guidance exists for institutions seeking
to build reproducible research capacity in applied settings. This thesis
delivers a working prototype infrastructure and implementation roadmap
specifically designed for the constraints and opportunities of
student-led, industry-partnered innovation research.

The research contributes:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Empirical evidence} of reproducibility challenges in applied
  supply chain sustainability research
\item
  \textbf{Theoretical insights} into the interplay of technical,
  organizational, and contextual barriers
\item
  \textbf{Practical deliverables} including a functional reproducible
  research infrastructure and implementation guidelines
\item
  \textbf{Institutional recommendations} for embedding reproducibility
  into educational and research workflows
\end{enumerate}

\section{1.6 Research Objectives and
Questions}\label{research-objectives-and-questions}

\subsection{Primary Objective}\label{primary-objective}

To investigate, implement, and document a reproducible research
framework for the Value Chain Hackers Lab that enables transparent,
verifiable, and cumulative knowledge development in supply chain
sustainability innovation.

\subsection{Research Questions}\label{research-questions}

\textbf{RQ1: To what extent can RStudio and Jupyter Notebooks enable
reproducible research in real-world supply chain sustainability
projects?}

This question examines the technical capabilities and limitations of
computational notebooks when applied to the messy realities of applied
innovation research. It considers both the theoretical affordances of
these tools and their practical performance given constraints of data
confidentiality, stakeholder complexity, and iterative research
processes.

\textbf{RQ2: What are the main technical, organizational, and contextual
obstacles to achieving reproducibility in this domain?}

This question identifies and categorizes barriers systematically,
distinguishing between: - Technical barriers (tool limitations,
infrastructure gaps) - Organizational barriers (institutional policies,
workflow standards, training deficiencies) - Contextual barriers (data
sensitivity, stakeholder dynamics, business constraints)

\textbf{RQ3: How do issues such as data confidentiality, tool
limitations, and context specificity affect the transferability and
credibility of reproducible research findings?}

This question examines second-order effects: even when research is
technically reproducible, how do business realities constrain the types
of transparency and verification that are feasible or desirable? It
considers the trade-offs inherent in applied research contexts.

\section{1.7 Methodology Overview}\label{methodology-overview}

This research employs a case study methodology with design science
components:

\textbf{Case study analysis}: Systematic retrospective analysis of five
completed VCH Lab tracks to identify reproducibility failures,
categorize barriers, and extract lessons learned.

\textbf{Design science}: Development and prototyping of a reproducible
research infrastructure integrating existing VCH Lab tools (JupyterHub,
Quarto, Git/GitHub, n8n) following Enterprise Data Reference Model
(EDRM) principles.

\textbf{Reproducibility audit}: Empirical testing of the infrastructure
by attempting to reproduce analysis using provided materials and
documentation.

\textbf{Stakeholder consultation}: Interviews with VCH Lab participants,
supervisors, IT staff, and industry partners to understand practical
barriers and requirements.

\textbf{Iterative refinement}: Multiple cycles of prototype development,
testing, feedback, and revision.

Detailed methodology is presented in Chapter 3.

\section{2.2 The Reproducibility Crisis in Science and Applied
Research}\label{the-reproducibility-crisis-in-science-and-applied-research}

\subsection{2.2.1 Defining
Reproducibility}\label{defining-reproducibility}

(\textbf{goodman2016?}) distinguish three forms of reproducibility:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Methods reproducibility}: Providing sufficient detail to
  enable independent researchers to repeat procedures
\item
  \textbf{Results reproducibility}: Obtaining consistent results using
  the same methods on independent data
\item
  \textbf{Inferential reproducibility}: Drawing consistent conclusions
  from either the same or different data
\end{itemize}

This thesis focuses primarily on methods reproducibility in
computational contexts: can an independent analyst regenerate reported
results using the provided code, data, and documentation?

\subsection{2.2.2 The Emergence of the Reproducibility
Crisis}\label{the-emergence-of-the-reproducibility-crisis}

The reproducibility crisis gained widespread attention through
(\textbf{baker2016?})'s survey of 1,500 scientists, revealing that 52\%
agreed there is a ``significant reproducibility crisis.'' Contributing
factors identified in the literature include:

\textbf{Publication bias}: Pressure to publish ``positive'' results
creates incentives to selectively report findings
(\textbf{ioannidis2005?})

\textbf{Methodological complexity}: Increasing sophistication of
computational analysis creates opportunities for errors and ambiguities
(\textbf{collberg2016?})

\textbf{Inadequate training}: Researchers receive limited education in
reproducible workflow practices (\textbf{sandve2013?})

\textbf{Tool proliferation}: Rapid evolution of software tools creates
dependency management challenges (\textbf{stodden2018?})

\textbf{Cultural norms}: Academic reward structures prioritize novelty
over verification (\textbf{nosek2015?})

\subsection{2.2.3 Reproducibility in Computational
Research}\label{reproducibility-in-computational-research}

(\textbf{stodden2018?}) conducted an empirical analysis of computational
reproducibility in published research, finding that even when code and
data are ``available,'' substantial researcher time and expertise are
required to reproduce results. Common barriers include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Missing dependencies or software versions
\item
  Inadequate documentation of computational environments
\item
  Proprietary software requirements
\item
  Unclear file organization
\item
  Undocumented data preprocessing steps
\end{itemize}

(\textbf{collberg2016?}) attempted to reproduce 613 computational
systems research papers, successfully building systems for only 32.1\%
of papers. Barriers included code unavailability, missing build
instructions, and undocumented dependencies.

\subsection{2.2.4 Reproducibility Beyond Traditional
Science}\label{reproducibility-beyond-traditional-science}

While most reproducibility literature focuses on academic research,
(\textbf{freire2012?}) examine reproducibility challenges in business
and government contexts, identifying additional constraints:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Data confidentiality}: Business-sensitive information cannot
  be publicly shared
\item
  \textbf{Stakeholder accountability}: Multiple parties have competing
  interests in research transparency
\item
  \textbf{Regulatory compliance}: Legal and ethical frameworks constrain
  data handling
\item
  \textbf{Cost structures}: Applied research often lacks funding for
  extensive documentation
\end{itemize}

These constraints require adapted reproducibility frameworks that
balance transparency ideals with practical realities.

\section{2.3 Solutions and Frameworks for Reproducible
Research}\label{solutions-and-frameworks-for-reproducible-research}

\subsection{2.3.1 The FAIR Principles}\label{the-fair-principles}

(\textbf{wilkinson2016?}) proposed the FAIR principles for scientific
data management:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Findable}: Persistent identifiers, rich metadata
\item
  \textbf{Accessible}: Retrievable via standard protocols
\item
  \textbf{Interoperable}: Standards-compliant formats and vocabularies
\item
  \textbf{Reusable}: Clear licensing, provenance, and documentation
\end{itemize}

Originally designed for data repositories, FAIR principles increasingly
guide broader reproducible research infrastructure development.

\subsection{2.3.2 Ten Simple Rules for Reproducible Computational
Research}\label{ten-simple-rules-for-reproducible-computational-research}

(\textbf{sandve2013?}) distilled practical guidance:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  For every result, keep track of how it was produced
\item
  Avoid manual data manipulation
\item
  Archive exact versions of all external programs
\item
  Version control all custom scripts
\item
  Record all intermediate results
\item
  For analyses including randomness, note underlying seeds
\item
  Store raw data behind plots
\item
  Generate hierarchical analysis output
\item
  Connect textual statements to underlying results
\item
  Provide public access to scripts, runs, and results
\end{enumerate}

These rules provide actionable steps for individual researchers but
assume supportive institutional infrastructure.

\subsection{2.3.3 Computational Notebooks as Reproducibility
Tools}\label{computational-notebooks-as-reproducibility-tools}

(\textbf{rule2019?}) analyze Jupyter Notebooks as reproducibility
infrastructure, proposing ``ten simple rules for writing and sharing
computational analyses in Jupyter Notebooks'':

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Tell a story for an audience
\item
  Document the process, not just the results
\item
  Use cell divisions to organize steps
\item
  Modularize code
\item
  Record dependencies
\item
  Use version control
\item
  Build a pipeline
\item
  Share and explain your data
\item
  Design your notebooks to be read, run, and explored
\item
  Advocate for open research
\end{enumerate}

(\textbf{kluyver2016?}) describe Jupyter's architecture and the
``computational narrative'' paradigm, emphasizing the integration of
explanation, code, and outputs.

\subsection{2.3.4 Infrastructure and Workflow
Tools}\label{infrastructure-and-workflow-tools}

Reproducible research requires integrated toolchains
(\textbf{stodden2018?}):

\textbf{Version control systems} (Git, GitHub) track changes to code and
documentation, enable collaboration, and provide history

\textbf{Environment management} (Docker, conda, renv) ensure
computational environments can be reconstructed

\textbf{Literate programming tools} (Jupyter, R Markdown, Quarto)
integrate code, narrative, and outputs

\textbf{Workflow automation} (Make, Snakemake, n8n) reduce manual steps
and document dependencies

\textbf{Data versioning} (DVC, Git-LFS) track changes to datasets

\textbf{Publication platforms} (GitHub Pages, Binder, Quarto Pub) share
interactive computational artifacts

\section{2.4 Supply Chain Sustainability Research: Challenges and
Opportunities}\label{supply-chain-sustainability-research-challenges-and-opportunities}

\subsection{2.4.1 The Imperative for Evidence-Based
Sustainability}\label{the-imperative-for-evidence-based-sustainability}

(\textbf{montabon2016?}) argue that ``making sustainability
sustainable'' requires rigorous validation of intervention
effectiveness. They identify common failures:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Confusing activity with impact
\item
  Relying on anecdotal evidence
\item
  Failing to measure long-term outcomes
\item
  Neglecting unintended consequences
\end{itemize}

Reproducible research practices address these failures by enabling
independent verification of sustainability claims.

\subsection{2.4.2 Methodological Pluralism in Supply Chain
Research}\label{methodological-pluralism-in-supply-chain-research}

Supply chain sustainability research typically integrates:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Quantitative analysis}: Life cycle assessment, carbon
  footprinting, cost-benefit analysis
\item
  \textbf{Qualitative investigation}: Stakeholder interviews, case
  studies, ethnography
\item
  \textbf{Participatory methods}: Co-design, action research,
  multi-stakeholder processes
\end{itemize}

This methodological diversity complicates reproducibility, as different
methods require different verification approaches
(\textbf{montabon2016?}).

\subsection{2.4.3 Data Challenges in Supply Chain
Research}\label{data-challenges-in-supply-chain-research}

Supply chain data are characterized by:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Distributed ownership}: Data held by multiple organizations
\item
  \textbf{Commercial sensitivity}: Competitive concerns limit sharing
\item
  \textbf{Heterogeneous formats}: Lack of standardization across systems
\item
  \textbf{Dynamic contexts}: Supply chains evolve rapidly
\end{itemize}

These characteristics require reproducibility frameworks that
accommodate partial transparency and contextual specificity
(\textbf{freire2012?}).

\section{2.5 Institutional and Organizational Barriers to Reproducible
Research}\label{institutional-and-organizational-barriers-to-reproducible-research}

\subsection{2.5.1 Academic Culture and Incentive
Structures}\label{academic-culture-and-incentive-structures}

(\textbf{nosek2015?}) analyze how academic reward systems prioritize
publication quantity and novelty over verification and robustness.
Career progression depends on ``positive'' results, creating
disincentives for time-intensive documentation and validation work.

(\textbf{munafo2017?}) call for a ``manifesto for reproducible
science,'' arguing that cultural change requires:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Protecting against cognitive biases
\item
  Improving methodological training
\item
  Implementing independent verification
\item
  Encouraging collaboration and data sharing
\item
  Aligning incentives with open practices
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.5.2 Educational Gaps}\label{educational-gaps}

(\textbf{baumer2014?}) survey data science education, finding that
reproducible workflow practices receive limited attention in curricula.
Students learn statistical methods and programming but not version
control, documentation standards, or workflow automation.

(\textbf{wilson2014?}) document ``best practices for scientific
computing'' but acknowledge that most researchers are self-taught
programmers lacking formal software engineering training.

\subsection{2.5.3 Institutional Infrastructure
Deficiencies}\label{institutional-infrastructure-deficiencies}

Even when individual researchers are motivated to adopt reproducible
practices, institutional barriers persist (\textbf{stodden2018?}):

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Lack of centralized data storage and compute infrastructure
\item
  Absence of technical support for reproducibility tools
\item
  Inadequate policies around data management and sharing
\item
  Limited training resources
\item
  Competing IT priorities
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.5.4 The ``Valley of Death'' Between Tools and
Adoption}\label{the-valley-of-death-between-tools-and-adoption}

(\textbf{freire2012?}) identify a persistent gap between the
availability of reproducibility tools and their actual adoption.
Barriers include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Learning curves}: Tools require substantial upfront investment
\item
  \textbf{Workflow disruption}: Adopting new practices interrupts
  established routines
\item
  \textbf{Perceived low return on investment}: Benefits accrue to others
  (readers, future researchers) rather than original authors
\item
  \textbf{Lack of community norms}: Without field-wide standards,
  individual adoption is costly
\end{itemize}

\section{2.6 Applied Research and Practice-Oriented
Science}\label{applied-research-and-practice-oriented-science}

\subsection{2.6.1 Characteristics of Applied Research
Contexts}\label{characteristics-of-applied-research-contexts}

Applied research differs from basic science in several dimensions
relevant to reproducibility:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Problem-driven}: Research questions emerge from practical
  challenges rather than theoretical gaps
\item
  \textbf{Stakeholder-embedded}: Multiple parties have interests in
  methods and findings
\item
  \textbf{Iterative and adaptive}: Methods evolve as understanding
  develops
\item
  \textbf{Time-constrained}: Deadlines may limit documentation
  thoroughness
\item
  \textbf{Multi-disciplinary}: Teams integrate diverse expertise and
  methodological traditions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{2.6.2 Universities of Applied Sciences
Context}\label{universities-of-applied-sciences-context}

Dutch universities of applied sciences (hogescholen) emphasize
``practice-oriented research'' bridging academic knowledge and
professional practice. Characteristics include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Student involvement}: Research often conducted by students as
  capstone projects
\item
  \textbf{Industry partnerships}: Research commissioned by external
  organizations
\item
  \textbf{Knowledge transfer focus}: Emphasis on actionable insights
  over theoretical contribution
\item
  \textbf{Short-term cycles}: Projects typically span 4-6 months
\end{itemize}

This context creates both opportunities (real-world relevance) and
challenges (limited resources, high turnover) for reproducible research.

\subsection{2.6.3 Innovation Labs as Research
Infrastructure}\label{innovation-labs-as-research-infrastructure}

Innovation labs provide dedicated spaces for collaborative, experimental
research. (\textbf{chesbrough2003?}) describe how ``open innovation''
models depend on knowledge sharing across organizational boundaries,
requiring transparent processes and documented artifacts.

The VCH Lab exemplifies this model, bringing together students, faculty,
and industry partners. However, (\textbf{freire2012?}) observe that
innovation labs often prioritize speed and creativity over documentation
and verification, creating reproducibility tensions.

\section{2.7 Enterprise Data Reference Model and Data
Governance}\label{enterprise-data-reference-model-and-data-governance}

\subsection{2.7.1 The EDRM Framework}\label{the-edrm-framework}

The Enterprise Data Reference Model provides architectural guidance for
managing data assets consistently across an organization. Core
principles include:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Standard definitions}: Common data vocabularies and ontologies
\item
  \textbf{Clear ownership}: Assigned responsibility for data quality and
  governance
\item
  \textbf{Lifecycle management}: Defined processes for data creation,
  use, archival, and deletion
\item
  \textbf{Quality assurance}: Validation and verification mechanisms
\item
  \textbf{Access control}: Security and privacy protections
\end{itemize}

While EDRM is typically applied in enterprise IT contexts, its
principles translate to reproducible research infrastructure design.

\subsection{2.7.2 ISO 27001 and Data
Security}\label{iso-27001-and-data-security}

ISO 27001 provides an information security management framework
addressing:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Confidentiality controls
\item
  Integrity assurance
\item
  Availability guarantees
\item
  Access management
\item
  Audit logging
\end{itemize}

For reproducible research in business contexts, security frameworks
enable appropriate transparency without compromising sensitive data
(\textbf{freire2012?}).

\section{2.8 Gaps in the Literature}\label{gaps-in-the-literature}

Synthesis of the literature reveals several gaps this thesis addresses:

\textbf{Empirical gap}: Most reproducibility studies examine academic
publications rather than applied research in innovation labs. Limited
systematic documentation exists of reproducibility barriers in
practice-oriented, student-led, industry-partnered research.

\textbf{Methodological gap}: Existing reproducibility frameworks (FAIR,
Open Science) are designed primarily for academic contexts with public
data sharing assumptions. Adaptation is needed for business innovation
contexts with confidentiality constraints.

\textbf{Practical gap}: While many studies identify reproducibility
barriers, fewer provide detailed implementation guidance for building
reproducible research capacity at institutional scale in
resource-constrained settings.

\textbf{Contextual gap}: Limited research examines reproducibility in
supply chain sustainability domains where methodological pluralism,
stakeholder complexity, and contextual specificity create unique
challenges.

\section{2.9 Theoretical Framework for This
Research}\label{theoretical-framework-for-this-research}

Drawing on the literature, this thesis adopts a multi-level framework
for understanding reproducibility:

\textbf{Technical level}: Tool capabilities, infrastructure, and
computational environments

\textbf{Methodological level}: Research design, documentation practices,

\textbf{Organizational level}: Institutional policies, training

\textbf{Cultural level}: Norms, incentives, and values shaping

Reproducibility emerges from alignment across these levels. Failures

\section{3.1 Research Design Overview}\label{research-design-overview}

This research employs a \textbf{case study methodology with design
science components}, combining retrospective analysis of existing VCH
Lab projects with prospective development and testing of a reproducible
research infrastructure.

\subsection{3.1.1 Rationale for Case Study
Approach}\label{rationale-for-case-study-approach}

(\textbf{yin2018?}) defines case study as ``an empirical method that
investigates a contemporary phenomenon in depth and within its
real-world context.'' Case study methodology is appropriate for this
research because:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Contextual complexity}: Reproducibility challenges are deeply
  embedded in institutional, organizational, and social contexts
\item
  \textbf{``How'' and ``why'' questions}: The research investigates how
  reproducibility failures occur and why certain barriers persist
\item
  \textbf{Limited control}: The researcher observes and analyzes
  existing practices rather than manipulating experimental variables
\item
  \textbf{Multiple evidence sources}: Case studies enable triangulation
  across documentation, interviews, and artifacts
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{3.1.2 Rationale for Design Science
Components}\label{rationale-for-design-science-components}

(\textbf{hevner2004?}) describe design science as ``fundamentally a
problem-solving paradigm'' that ``creates and evaluates IT artifacts
intended to solve identified organizational problems.'' Design science
complements case study by:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Producing actionable artifacts}: Creating infrastructure
  rather than only documenting problems
\item
  \textbf{Iterative refinement}: Enabling cycles of prototyping,
  testing, and improvement
\item
  \textbf{Demonstration of feasibility}: Proving that proposed solutions
  actually work in context
\item
  \textbf{Knowledge contribution}: Generating both prescriptive design
  principles and empirical insights
\end{enumerate}

The combination enables both \textbf{diagnosis} (what barriers exist?)
and \textbf{solution development} (how can they be addressed?).

\section{3.6 Reproducibility Audit
Methodology}\label{reproducibility-audit-methodology}

To validate the infrastructure, a reproducibility audit tested whether
an independent researcher could reproduce analysis following provided
documentation.

\subsection{3.6.1 Audit Protocol}\label{audit-protocol}

\textbf{Step 1: Environment setup} - Follow documentation to configure
computational environment - Install dependencies - Verify access to data
and code

\textbf{Step 2: Execution} - Run provided notebooks/scripts - Compare
generated outputs to documented results - Document any errors or
discrepancies

\textbf{Step 3: Verification} - Check that numerical results match
(within floating-point tolerance) - Verify that visualizations are
substantially equivalent - Confirm that conclusions are supported by
reproduced outputs

\textbf{Step 4: Documentation assessment} - Evaluate clarity and
completeness of instructions - Identify missing information or
ambiguities - Suggest improvements

\subsection{3.6.2 Auditor Selection}\label{auditor-selection}

The reproducibility auditor was selected based on: - Competent in Python
or R programming - Familiar with Jupyter/RStudio but not with this
specific project - Available to invest 4-6 hours in reproduction attempt
- Willing to provide detailed feedback

\subsection{3.6.3 Success Criteria}\label{success-criteria}

Reproducibility was considered successful if: - Auditor could set up
environment without external assistance - Code executed without errors -
Numerical outputs matched within 1\% tolerance - Qualitative outputs
(visualizations, tables) were substantially equivalent - Auditor
confirmed they understood the analytical process

\section{3.9 Limitations of the
Methodology}\label{limitations-of-the-methodology}

Several methodological limitations should be acknowledged:

\textbf{Retrospective case analysis:} - Reliance on existing
documentation limits insight into processes - Student unavailability
limits understanding of researcher intentions - Cannot observe real-time
decision-making

\textbf{Single-institution focus:} - Findings specific to VCH Lab
context - Transferability to other settings requires caution - May not
represent broader applied research landscape

\textbf{Limited reproducibility audit:} - Single auditor rather than
multiple independent replications - Short timeframe for audit - Auditor
known to researcher (mitigated by protocol rigor)

\textbf{Researcher embeddedness:} - Potential bias toward confirming
infrastructure value - Dual role as researcher and infrastructure
developer - Limited critical distance

\textbf{Technical scope:} - Focus on computational reproducibility, not
full replication - Emphasis on quantitative/computational methods -
Limited attention to qualitative research reproducibility

These limitations are addressed through triangulation, explicit
acknowledgment, and careful claims about generalizability.

\subsection{4.7.2 Typology of Barriers}\label{typology-of-barriers}

Barriers cluster into three categories:

\textbf{Technical barriers:} - Absence of version control infrastructure
- Dependence on ephemeral commercial tools - Lack of institutional data
storage and management systems - Inadequate technical support for
reproducibility tools

\textbf{Organizational barriers:} - Educational model prioritizing
outputs over process - Assessment criteria not rewarding documentation -
Missing quality assurance checkpoints - Short project timelines creating
time pressure - High turnover preventing knowledge accumulation

\textbf{Contextual barriers:} - Data confidentiality constraints (though
not observed in sampled cases) - Stakeholder sensitivities limiting full
transparency - Diverse methodological traditions lacking common
standards - Tension between creative innovation and methodological rigor

\subsection{4.7.3 Root Cause Analysis}\label{root-cause-analysis}

Deeper analysis identifies \textbf{systemic rather than individual
causes}:

\textbf{Structural cause:} The VCH Lab's educational model does not
embed reproducibility requirements into project design, execution, or
assessment. Students are neither taught reproducible practices nor
rewarded for implementing them.

\textbf{Cultural cause:} Innovation culture emphasizes speed,
creativity, and stakeholder satisfaction---qualities that can conflict
with time-intensive documentation and verification processes.

\textbf{Resource cause:} VCH Lab lacks dedicated technical
infrastructure, staff support, and institutional policies for
reproducible research. Individual students cannot create reproducible
workflows without these supports.

\textbf{Pedagogical cause:} Theory U and design thinking frameworks
emphasize iterative emergence but do not specify documentation
practices. Research methods are assumed rather than taught.

\subsection{4.7.4 Consequences for VCH
Lab}\label{consequences-for-vch-lab}

These failures create multiple negative consequences:

\textbf{Knowledge loss:} Each project cohort ``reinvents the wheel''
because prior work cannot be accessed or built upon

\textbf{Credibility risk:} Industry partners and external evaluators
cannot verify research quality

\textbf{Wasted effort:} Time invested in research generates no
cumulative institutional knowledge

\textbf{Student disadvantage:} Graduates lack portfolio evidence of
rigorous research capabilities

\textbf{Institutional reputation:} University cannot demonstrate
research quality to accreditors or funders

\textbf{Sustainability impact:} Inability to verify sustainability
claims undermines mission

Findings align with broader reproducibility literature:

\textbf{Consistent with (\textbf{stodden2018?}):} Even when data and
code nominally ``exist,'' inadequate documentation prevents reproduction

\textbf{Consistent with (\textbf{collberg2016?}):} Missing dependencies,

\textbf{Extends (\textbf{freire2012?}):} Applied research in business

sustainability innovation lab

\section{5.1 Introduction: From Diagnosis to
Solution}\label{introduction-from-diagnosis-to-solution}

Chapter 4 documented systematic reproducibility failures across VCH Lab
research tracks, identifying technical, organizational, and contextual
barriers. This chapter presents the reproducible research infrastructure
designed to address these failures, applying Enterprise Data Reference
Model (EDRM) principles and integrating existing VCH Lab tools
(JupyterHub, Quarto, Git/GitHub, n8n).

The infrastructure aims to: 1. \textbf{Enable} reproducibility through
appropriate tools and workflows 2. \textbf{Enforce} standards through
templates and automation 3. \textbf{Educate} through embedded guidance
and examples 4. \textbf{Scale} across multiple simultaneous VCH Lab
projects

\section{5.2 Design Principles and
Architecture}\label{design-principles-and-architecture}

\subsection{5.2.1 Core Design Principles}\label{core-design-principles}

The infrastructure follows eight guiding principles:

\textbf{1. Default reproducibility} Workflows automatically produce
documented outputs rather than requiring extra effort for documentation

\textbf{2. Progressive complexity} Start simple for beginners, provide
advanced features for experienced users

\textbf{3. Tool integration} Seamless connections between Git, Jupyter,
Quarto minimize friction

\textbf{4. Institutional persistence} Research artifacts stored
independently of individual student accounts

\textbf{5. Appropriate transparency} Support both open research and
confidential work as needed

\textbf{6. Educational scaffolding} Templates and examples teach
reproducible practices through use

\textbf{7. Stakeholder accessibility} Outputs comprehensible to industry
partners, not just technical audiences

\textbf{8. Continuous improvement} Infrastructure designed for iterative
refinement based on user feedback

\subsection{5.4.3 Technical Challenges}\label{technical-challenges}

\textbf{Challenge 1: Dependency management}

Problem: Students used different package versions, breaking
reproducibility

Solution: Automated environment generation with version pinning -
Pre-commit hook checks environment files are updated when imports change
- Clear instructions for adding dependencies - Regular environment
testing in CI/CD

\textbf{Challenge 2: Large file handling}

Problem: Students committed large datasets directly to Git, causing
repository bloat

Solution: Git LFS configuration and monitoring - \texttt{.gitattributes}
configured for common large file types - n8n workflow alerts when large
files detected - Documentation on appropriate data storage locations

\textbf{Challenge 3: Notebook execution state}

Problem: Notebooks executed out-of-order created misleading results

Solution: Multiple safeguards - Pre-commit hook clears notebook outputs
- CI/CD runs notebooks top-to-bottom - Quarto freeze feature caches
stable results

\textbf{Challenge 4: Documentation burden}

Problem: Students viewed documentation as ``extra work'' rather than
integral to research

Solution: Embedded documentation - Templates integrate documentation
into workflow - Automated extraction of documentation for reports -
Gamification: progress dashboard showing documentation completeness

\section{5.7 Limitations and
Challenges}\label{limitations-and-challenges}

\subsection{5.7.1 Technical Limitations}\label{technical-limitations}

\textbf{Does not solve:} - Qualitative research reproducibility
(requires additional methodology) - Confidentiality conflicts
(infrastructure supports confidential work but cannot eliminate
tensions) - External dependencies (APIs, datasets outside VCH Lab
control) - Long-term format obsolescence (requires ongoing curation)

\textbf{Partial solutions:} - Qualitative templates provide starting
point but require methodological training - Confidentiality
classification and access control enable appropriate transparency -
Dependency documentation and containerization mitigate external
dependencies - Open formats and regular migrations address obsolescence

\subsection{5.7.2 Organizational
Challenges}\label{organizational-challenges}

\textbf{Cultural resistance:} - Some students perceive documentation as
``busywork'' - Industry partners may prioritize speed over rigor -
Faculty accustomed to existing (non-reproducible) practices

\textbf{Mitigation strategies:} - Emphasize benefits (efficiency,
portfolio quality, credibility) - Provide templates reducing
documentation burden - Demonstrate value through pilot project success -
Integrate reproducibility into assessment criteria

\textbf{Resource constraints:} - IT support capacity limited - Training
time competes with project execution - Coordinator oversight required
(not fully automated)

\textbf{Mitigation strategies:} - Prioritize automation to minimize
manual tasks - Integrate training into existing onboarding - Leverage
student peer mentorship - Document infrastructure to enable self-service

\subsection{5.7.3 Pedagogical Tensions}\label{pedagogical-tensions}

\textbf{Innovation vs.~rigor tradeoff:} - Design thinking emphasizes
rapid prototyping and emergence - Reproducibility requires deliberate
documentation and structure - Can these coexist?

\textbf{Resolution:} - Differentiate exploration phase (more flexible)
from validation phase (more structured) - Emphasize that documentation
accelerates iteration by providing clear record of what was tried -
Frame reproducibility as design constraint enhancing creativity (like
accessibility guidelines)

\section{5.8 Comparison with Alternative
Approaches}\label{comparison-with-alternative-approaches}

Several alternative approaches to reproducible research infrastructure
exist:

\subsection{5.8.1 Commercial Platforms}\label{commercial-platforms}

\textbf{Examples:} Databricks, Google Colab, Observable

\textbf{Advantages:} - Polished user interfaces - Integrated
collaboration features - Cloud-based, no local infrastructure required

\textbf{Disadvantages:} - Licensing costs (prohibitive at scale) -
Vendor lock-in - Data sovereignty concerns - Limited customization

\textbf{VCH Lab choice:} Open-source tools provide greater flexibility,
no recurring costs, and alignment with open science values

\subsection{5.8.2 Institutional Repository
Only}\label{institutional-repository-only}

\textbf{Approach:} Require final deliverables deposited in university
repository

\textbf{Advantages:} - Simple to implement - Preserves final outputs -
Discoverable via library systems

\textbf{Disadvantages:} - Does not ensure reproducibility (process still
opaque) - Post-hoc archiving, not integrated workflow - Static
snapshots, not versioned development

\textbf{VCH Lab choice:} Institutional repository used for long-term
archiving, but integrated workflow infrastructure provides greater
reproducibility assurance

\subsection{5.8.3 Fully Manual
Documentation}\label{fully-manual-documentation}

\textbf{Approach:} Students document work in Word/PDF reports

\textbf{Advantages:} - Familiar tools - No technical learning curve -
Immediate start

\textbf{Disadvantages:} - Narrative description cannot replace
executable code - Manual transcription introduces errors - No version
control - Cannot verify computational results

\textbf{VCH Lab choice:} Computational notebooks integrate narrative,
code, and outputs, providing verifiability impossible with static
documents

\section{6.2 Research Question 1: Capabilities and Limitations of
Computational
Notebooks}\label{research-question-1-capabilities-and-limitations-of-computational-notebooks}

\textbf{RQ1: To what extent can RStudio and Jupyter Notebooks enable
reproducible research in real-world supply chain sustainability
projects?}

\subsection{6.2.1 Technical Capabilities
Demonstrated}\label{technical-capabilities-demonstrated}

The infrastructure implementation and reproducibility audit demonstrate
that computational notebooks possess significant technical capabilities
for enabling reproducible research:

\textbf{Successful integration of narrative and computation:} The pilot
project successfully used Jupyter Notebooks to create ``computational
narratives'' (\textbf{kluyver2016?}) that integrated explanatory text,
executable code, and visualizations. The reproducibility auditor
confirmed that this integration made the analytical process transparent
and understandable, even across disciplinary boundaries.

\textbf{Environment reproducibility:} Using Conda environment
specifications (\texttt{environment.yml}) and Docker containerization,
the infrastructure achieved computational environment reproducibility.
The auditor successfully recreated the exact software environment 6
months after project completion, demonstrating that
(\textbf{stodden2018?})'s concerns about dependency management can be
addressed through appropriate tooling.

\textbf{Executable documentation:} Quarto's ability to render notebooks
into publication-quality documents creates ``living documentation'' that
stays synchronized with code. When analysis was updated during the pilot
project, documentation automatically reflected changes, preventing the
documentation drift that plagued the Cacao track case study.

\textbf{Version control integration:} Git integration enabled
transparent tracking of analytical evolution. The pilot project
repository contained 147 commits over 12 weeks, creating a complete
audit trail of research decisions. This addresses
(\textbf{collberg2016?})'s finding that most computational research
lacks traceable provenance.

\subsection{6.2.2 Limitations
Encountered}\label{limitations-encountered}

However, the research also revealed significant limitations:

\textbf{Learning curve barriers:} Pilot project students reported that
the initial learning investment was ``steep'' and ``intimidating.'' Two
of three students had no prior Git experience. Even with templates and
training, the first 2-3 weeks involved substantial troubleshooting. This
confirms (\textbf{wilson2014?})'s observation that reproducible research
tools require skills not typically taught in business programs.

\textbf{Qualitative research challenges:} Computational notebooks excel
at quantitative analysis but provide limited support for qualitative
research reproducibility. The Bakery track's interview analysis would
not have been substantially improved by Jupyter Notebooks
alone---transcription, coding, and thematic analysis require different
workflows. (\textbf{freire2012?})'s framework for reproducibility must
extend beyond computational methods to encompass qualitative rigor.

\textbf{Stakeholder accessibility:} While the reproducibility auditor (a
technical peer) successfully navigated the infrastructure, industry
partners found the GitHub repository and notebook format ``confusing''
and ``too technical.'' Stakeholders preferred traditional PDF reports.
This reveals a tension: reproducibility infrastructure designed for
scientific verification may not align with stakeholder communication
needs.

\textbf{Incomplete automation:} Despite n8n workflow automation, several
reproducibility checkpoints required manual intervention. Students
occasionally forgot to update metadata, committed large files, or
executed notebooks out-of-order despite automated checks. Full
reproducibility required vigilant oversight, not just technical
infrastructure.

\subsection{6.2.3 Contextual
Dependencies}\label{contextual-dependencies}

The effectiveness of computational notebooks depends heavily on context:

\textbf{Project characteristics:} - \textbf{Computational intensity}:
Projects involving substantial data analysis benefited greatly; projects
involving primarily literature synthesis or stakeholder consultation
showed limited benefit - \textbf{Team size}: Solo students found
notebooks helpful for self-documentation; larger teams benefited more
from collaborative features but also experienced more merge conflicts -
\textbf{Timeline}: Longer projects (\textgreater4 months) showed greater
ROI on documentation investment; shorter projects struggled to justify
learning curve

\textbf{Organizational support:} - \textbf{Technical infrastructure}:
JupyterHub provided centralized, consistent environment; projects
lacking institutional compute resources would face barriers -
\textbf{Expertise availability}: VCH Lab coordinator's technical
proficiency enabled rapid troubleshooting; labs lacking such expertise
would struggle - \textbf{Policy alignment}: Integration with assessment
criteria and mandatory templates created adoption pressure; voluntary
adoption would likely fail

\subsection{6.2.4 Answering RQ1: Conditional
Enablement}\label{answering-rq1-conditional-enablement}

\textbf{Conclusion for RQ1:}

Computational notebooks \emph{can} enable reproducible research in
supply chain sustainability projects, but enablement is
\textbf{conditional} rather than automatic. Necessary conditions
include:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Appropriate research methods}: Computational analysis, data
  processing, modeling (not purely qualitative or strategic work)
\item
  \textbf{Institutional infrastructure}: Centralized compute
  environment, version control system, technical support
\item
  \textbf{Educational scaffolding}: Training, templates, examples, and
  mentorship
\item
  \textbf{Organizational incentives}: Assessment integration, policy
  requirements, visible benefits
\item
  \textbf{Adequate timeline}: Sufficient project duration to justify
  learning investment
\end{enumerate}

When these conditions are met, notebooks provide powerful
reproducibility capabilities. When conditions are absent, notebooks
become barriers rather than enablers.

This finding extends (\textbf{rule2019?})'s ``ten simple rules'' by
emphasizing that rules alone are insufficient---institutional ecosystem
matters as much as individual practices.

\section{6.3 Research Question 2: Barriers to
Reproducibility}\label{research-question-2-barriers-to-reproducibility}

\textbf{RQ2: What are the main technical, organizational, and contextual
obstacles to achieving reproducibility in this domain?}

\subsection{6.3.1 Technical Barriers: Infrastructure
Gaps}\label{technical-barriers-infrastructure-gaps}

Case study analysis revealed multiple technical barriers:

\textbf{Absence of version control infrastructure:} All five case study
tracks lacked version control. Students saved files locally, shared via
email or cloud drives, and had no history of changes. This resulted in
``version chaos'' (CRM track: 7 presentation versions with no coherent
history) and inability to trace decision-making.

\textbf{Root cause}: Git was available but not required, not taught, and
not integrated into workflow. Students defaulted to familiar but
inadequate tools.

\textbf{Ephemeral storage platforms:} Textile track demonstrated
complete data loss due to dependence on commercial creative tools
(Canva, Miro) tied to student accounts. When accounts expired, projects
vanished.

\textbf{Root cause}: No institutional policy requiring durable storage
formats or archival procedures. Students optimized for immediate
convenience rather than long-term preservation.

\textbf{Tool proliferation without integration:} Students used
disconnected tools (Word for writing, Excel for data, PowerPoint for
presentations, email for sharing) creating fragmented workflows with
manual transcription between systems---each transcription introducing
potential errors.

\textbf{Root cause}: Lack of integrated reproducible research platform
guiding students toward coherent toolchain.

\textbf{Inadequate technical support:} When students encountered
technical problems (Git merge conflicts, Jupyter kernel crashes,
dependency errors), resolution required specialized knowledge
unavailable within teams.

\textbf{Root cause}: VCH Lab IT support focused on general
infrastructure (network, accounts) rather than research-specific tools.
No dedicated research computing support role.

\subsection{6.3.2 Organizational Barriers: Missing
Structure}\label{organizational-barriers-missing-structure}

Organizational barriers proved more significant than technical ones:

\textbf{Educational model misalignment:} The VCH Lab pedagogy emphasizes
Theory U and design thinking---frameworks prioritizing observation,
sensing, and creative emergence. Neither framework includes explicit
reproducibility or documentation requirements.

As one student reflected: ``We were never told documentation mattered
for the grade, so we focused on making the final presentation look
good.''

\textbf{Root cause}: Pedagogical philosophy values innovation outputs
over research process transparency. Reproducibility framed as optional
``best practice'' rather than core competency.

\textbf{Assessment criteria gaps:} Analysis of GP1 assessment rubrics
revealed that methodological transparency and verifiability are
mentioned but constitute small fractions of total points. Students
rationally allocate effort to criteria carrying most weight (problem
definition, solution creativity, stakeholder impact).

\textbf{Root cause}: Assessment design reflects historical norms where
applied research quality was judged by practitioner acceptance rather
than scientific reproducibility.

\textbf{Short project timelines:} Four-month projects create pressure to
``show results'' quickly. Documentation is perceived as delaying
tangible progress. As the CRM track demonstrated, teams prioritize
concept development over process recording.

\textbf{Root cause}: Academic calendar constraints and desire to
maximize student ``value delivery'' to industry partners.

\textbf{High student turnover:} Knowledge accumulated during one project
cohort is lost when students graduate. Each cohort ``reinvents''
research practices. Institutional learning cannot accumulate.

\textbf{Root cause}: Structural characteristic of educational research
labs---unavoidable but can be mitigated through persistent documentation
infrastructure.

\textbf{No quality assurance checkpoints:} Projects lacked interim
reviews focused on research quality. Supervisors reviewed ``progress''
(what results emerged) but not ``process'' (how results were derived).
Problems only became visible at final submission.

\textbf{Root cause}: Limited faculty time for intensive project
oversight; assumption that students possess research skills from prior
coursework (often unfounded).

\subsection{6.3.3 Contextual Barriers: Domain-Specific
Challenges}\label{contextual-barriers-domain-specific-challenges}

Supply chain sustainability research presents unique challenges:

\textbf{Stakeholder confidentiality:} Industry partners often requested
that specific company data, strategic plans, or competitive information
not be publicly disclosed. While the five case studies did not exhibit
confidentiality conflicts (due to their limited data collection), pilot
project participants noted tension between transparency ideals and
partner expectations.

\textbf{Implication}: Reproducible research frameworks must accommodate
graduated transparency---methods and code can be open while preserving
data confidentiality through synthetic data, aggregation, or access
restrictions.

\textbf{Methodological pluralism:} Supply chain sustainability research
integrates life cycle assessment, stakeholder interviews, systems
modeling, regulatory analysis, and participatory design. No single
reproducibility standard spans this methodological diversity.

\textbf{Implication}: Infrastructure must be flexible, supporting
diverse methods rather than enforcing single approach. Templates provide
starting points but cannot be prescriptive.

\textbf{Contextual specificity:} Supply chain interventions are deeply
context-dependent. An effective practice in Dutch bakeries may not
transfer to West African cacao supply chains. Reproducibility
(generating same results in same context) differs from generalizability
(transferring findings across contexts).

\textbf{Implication}: Reproducibility documentation must make context
explicit---describing boundary conditions, assumptions, and scope
limitations enabling appropriate transfer rather than naive replication.

\textbf{Rapid evolution:} Supply chains and regulatory environments
change quickly. Research completed in 2023 references regulations
amended by 2024. ``Reproducibility'' across time becomes challenging
when external landscape shifts.

\textbf{Implication}: Version control and timestamp documentation create
temporal reproducibility---ability to understand what was true at
research time, even if no longer current.

\subsection{6.3.4 Interactions Among Barrier
Types}\label{interactions-among-barrier-types}

Barriers compound rather than simply adding:

\textbf{Technical-organizational interaction:} Version control
infrastructure (technical) is only useful if organizational policies
require its use and students receive training in its operation. VCH
Lab's JupyterHub (technical capability) was underutilized due to lack of
training and integration into workflows (organizational gap).

\textbf{Organizational-contextual interaction:} Short timelines
(organizational constraint) become more problematic when research
methods are diverse and context-specific (contextual characteristics),
requiring more rather than less documentation time.

\textbf{Technical-contextual interaction:} Tools optimized for
computational reproducibility (Jupyter Notebooks) are less suited to
qualitative methods common in sustainability research (contextual
characteristic), requiring additional tool development (technical gap).

\subsection{6.3.5 Answering RQ2: Systemic Barrier
Architecture}\label{answering-rq2-systemic-barrier-architecture}

\textbf{Conclusion for RQ2:}

Barriers to reproducibility form a \textbf{systemic architecture} rather
than isolated obstacles:

\textbf{Technical barriers} (version control absence, ephemeral storage,
tool fragmentation) are necessary but not sufficient explanations. They
could be addressed through infrastructure investment.

\textbf{Organizational barriers} (misaligned pedagogy, inadequate
assessment criteria, limited oversight) are deeper and more
consequential. They shape whether technical capabilities are adopted.

\textbf{Contextual barriers} (confidentiality, methodological diversity,
contextual specificity) are intrinsic to the domain and require adapted
reproducibility frameworks rather than elimination.

\textbf{Key insight}: Barriers interact multiplicatively. Addressing
technical barriers alone (providing Git repository) fails if
organizational context does not require version control and students
lack training. Addressing organizational barriers (changing assessment)
fails if technical infrastructure is absent.

\textbf{Implication for intervention design}: Reproducibility solutions
must be \textbf{holistic}, integrating technical infrastructure,
organizational policy, educational training, and contextual adaptation
simultaneously.

This finding validates (\textbf{munafo2017?})'s ``manifesto for
reproducible science'' argument that cultural and systemic change is
necessary---individual tools or training are insufficient.

\section{6.4 Research Question 3: Confidentiality, Tool Limitations, and
Transferability}\label{research-question-3-confidentiality-tool-limitations-and-transferability}

\textbf{RQ3: How do issues such as data confidentiality, tool
limitations, and context specificity affect the transferability and
credibility of reproducible research findings?}

\subsection{6.4.1 Data Confidentiality: Graduated
Transparency}\label{data-confidentiality-graduated-transparency}

The research revealed that confidentiality need not eliminate
reproducibility, but requires \textbf{graduated transparency}:

\textbf{Levels of transparency:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{Full transparency}: Methods, code, data, and outputs all
  public (ideal for publicly-funded research with non-sensitive data)
\item
  \textbf{Methods transparency}: Research design, analytical procedures,
  and code public; data access restricted (suitable for
  business-sensitive data)
\item
  \textbf{Process transparency}: Internal documentation enables
  institutional reproduction even if external sharing prohibited
  (minimum for proprietary research)
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Implementation in infrastructure:}

The VCH Lab infrastructure implemented graduated transparency through:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Classification system}: Projects tagged as Public, Internal,
  or Confidential
\item
  \textbf{Repository access control}: Public repositories for
  non-sensitive work, private repositories for confidential projects
\item
  \textbf{Synthetic data substitution}: Templates for generating
  synthetic datasets matching statistical properties of confidential
  data, enabling external verification of analytical procedures even
  when real data cannot be shared
\item
  \textbf{Embargo periods}: Temporary restrictions with scheduled public
  release
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Findings from pilot project:}

Industry partner initially concerned about ``open research'' accepted
Internal classification with understanding that: - Methods and code
would be open (demonstrating rigor) - Company-specific data would remain
confidential - Aggregated findings publishable (contributing to
knowledge) - Competitive insights protected

This graduated approach maintained reproducibility benefits (transparent
methods, verifiable analysis) while addressing legitimate
confidentiality concerns.

\subsection{6.4.2 Tool Limitations: Workflow Integration
Challenges}\label{tool-limitations-workflow-integration-challenges}

Tool limitations manifested in three areas:

\textbf{Qualitative analysis gaps:}

Jupyter Notebooks and RStudio are optimized for computational analysis.
Qualitative research involving interview transcription, thematic coding,
and interpretive synthesis requires different tools (NVivo, Atlas.ti,
MaxQDA) with limited reproducibility affordances.

\textbf{Attempted solution}: Integration of qualitative data management
templates (interview documentation, coding frameworks) into the
infrastructure, alongside computational tools. This hybrid approach
acknowledges that no single tool solves all reproducibility challenges.

\textbf{Result}: Partial success. Templates improved qualitative
documentation but did not achieve computational notebook-level
integration. Qualitative reproducibility remains an open challenge
requiring further development.

\textbf{Visual design constraints:}

Supply chain projects often require visual communication (infographics,
stakeholder maps, process diagrams). While tools like Canva and Miro
excel at visual design, they lack reproducibility features (version
control, scriptable generation, format durability).

\textbf{Attempted solution}: Encourage use of code-generated
visualizations (Python matplotlib, R ggplot2, JavaScript D3.js) where
possible. For necessary graphic design, require export to durable
formats (SVG, PDF) with design files archived.

\textbf{Result}: Tension between design quality and reproducibility.
Code-generated visualizations often less polished than manual designs.
Students gravitate toward visual tools, requiring explicit intervention
to preserve artifacts.

\textbf{Collaboration friction:}

Git's text-based diff and merge work well for code but poorly for
notebooks (JSON format creates false conflicts) and not at all for
binary files (Word documents, Excel spreadsheets).

\textbf{Attempted solution}: Tools like nbdime for notebook diffing,
jupytext for markdown conversion, and move toward plain-text workflows
where possible.

\textbf{Result}: Improved but not eliminated friction. Merge conflicts
remain confusing for novice users.

\subsection{6.4.3 Context Specificity: Documentation of Boundary
Conditions}\label{context-specificity-documentation-of-boundary-conditions}

The research found that context specificity does not prevent
reproducibility but requires \textbf{explicit boundary condition
documentation}:

\textbf{What makes findings context-specific:}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Geographic factors}: Dutch regulatory environment, European
  supply chain structure, local industry norms
\item
  \textbf{Temporal factors}: Current state of technology, recent
  regulatory changes, market conditions
\item
  \textbf{Organizational factors}: Company size, maturity, culture,
  capabilities
\item
  \textbf{Methodological factors}: Available data, research access,
  stakeholder cooperation
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Infrastructure approach:}

The \texttt{project-metadata.yml} template requires explicit
documentation of:

\AttributeTok{  }\FunctionTok{geographic\_scope}\KeywordTok{:}\AttributeTok{ }\StringTok{"Netherlands, focus on Amsterdam region"}

\textbf{Effect on transferability:}

Explicit context documentation enables \textbf{informed transfer} rather
than naive replication:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Researchers in other contexts understand what might and might not
  transfer
\item
  Comparative studies can systematically examine how context affects
  outcomes
\item
  Meta-analyses can account for contextual variation
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Effect on credibility:}

Counter-intuitively, acknowledging context specificity \textbf{enhances}
rather than diminishes credibility:

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Demonstrates researcher awareness of limitations
\item
  Signals scientific humility rather than overconfidence
\item
  Enables critical evaluation by readers
\item
  Prevents false generalization
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6.4.4 Trade-offs and Pragmatic
Balancing}\label{trade-offs-and-pragmatic-balancing}

The research revealed inherent trade-offs requiring pragmatic balancing:

\textbf{Trade-off 1: Transparency vs.~Confidentiality}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Pure transparency maximizes verifiability but may be inappropriate or
  illegal for sensitive data
\item
  Pure confidentiality protects interests but eliminates independent
  verification
\item
  \textbf{Balance}: Graduated transparency with methods open, data
  appropriately protected
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trade-off 2: Tool sophistication vs.~Accessibility}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Advanced reproducibility tools (Git, Docker, CI/CD) provide robust
  infrastructure but have steep learning curves
\item
  Simple tools (shared drives, email) are accessible but lack
  reproducibility features
\item
  \textbf{Balance}: Graduated tooling with templates and examples
  lowering barriers to advanced tools
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trade-off 3: Documentation completeness vs.~Resource
constraints}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Complete documentation of every decision and assumption would require
  unlimited time
\item
  No documentation creates knowledge loss and irreproducibility
\item
  \textbf{Balance}: Mandatory minimum documentation (templates,
  metadata) with optional deeper documentation for complex decisions
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Trade-off 4: Standardization vs.~Flexibility}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Rigid standards ensure consistency but may not fit diverse research
  methods
\item
  Complete flexibility accommodates diversity but prevents comparison
  and integration
\item
  \textbf{Balance}: Core standards (metadata, version control, file
  organization) with methodological flexibility within structure
\end{itemize}

\subsection{6.4.5 Answering RQ3: Constructive
Trade-offs}\label{answering-rq3-constructive-trade-offs}

\textbf{Conclusion for RQ3:}

Data confidentiality, tool limitations, and context specificity
\textbf{do not prevent reproducibility} but require \textbf{constructive
trade-offs}:

\textbf{Confidentiality} → \textbf{Graduated transparency}: Methods and
analytical procedures can be transparent even when data must be
protected. Synthetic data, aggregation, access restrictions, and embargo
periods enable appropriate transparency.

\textbf{Tool limitations} → \textbf{Hybrid infrastructure}: No single
tool solves all reproducibility challenges. Infrastructure must
integrate computational notebooks (quantitative), documentation
templates (qualitative), version control (all), and appropriate exports
from visual tools.

\textbf{Context specificity} → \textbf{Explicit boundary documentation}:
Rather than seeking context-independent findings (often impossible in
applied sustainability research), reproducibility requires making
context explicit so others can understand transferability scope.

\textbf{Impact on credibility}: These accommodations to real-world
constraints \textbf{enhance} rather than diminish credibility, by
demonstrating: - Awareness of ethical responsibilities (confidentiality)
- Methodological sophistication (appropriate tool selection) -
Scientific humility (acknowledging limitations)

\textbf{Key insight}: ``Perfect'' reproducibility (full transparency,
universal tools, context-free findings) is often neither possible nor
desirable in applied research. ``Appropriate'' reproducibility
(transparent within constraints, integrated tools, explicit context) is
both achievable and valuable.

This extends (\textbf{freire2012?})'s observation that reproducibility
in business contexts requires adapted frameworks, not abandoned
standards.

\section{6.5 Cross-Cutting Themes and
Insights}\label{cross-cutting-themes-and-insights}

Beyond the specific research questions, several cross-cutting themes
emerged:

\subsection{6.5.1 The Primacy of Organizational
Culture}\label{the-primacy-of-organizational-culture}

\textbf{Finding}: Technical infrastructure is necessary but not
sufficient for reproducibility. Organizational culture determines
whether infrastructure is adopted.

\textbf{Evidence}: - VCH Lab had access to Git for years but no one used
it until made mandatory - JupyterHub was underutilized until integrated
into project templates - Pilot project succeeded largely due to
coordinator commitment and assessment integration

\textbf{Implication}: Reproducibility initiatives must address culture
change through: - Visible leadership commitment - Assessment alignment
(grade what you value) - Success stories and champions - Community norms
(``this is how we do research here'')

\textbf{Connection to literature}: Confirms (\textbf{nosek2015?})'s
argument that scientific culture reform is central to reproducibility
progress.

\subsection{6.5.2 The Documentation
Paradox}\label{the-documentation-paradox}

\textbf{Finding}: Documentation is simultaneously the most important
reproducibility practice and the least intrinsically rewarding.

\textbf{Paradox components}: - \textbf{Temporal asymmetry}:
Documentation costs borne immediately (during busy project) but benefits
accrue later (to future researchers, auditors, one's future self) -
\textbf{Beneficiary asymmetry}: Documenter bears costs, but others
(reproducers, reviewers) capture benefits - \textbf{Visibility
asymmetry}: Undocumented research looks similar to documented research
in final presentations---quality difference invisible to casual
observers

\textbf{Implication}: Cannot rely on intrinsic motivation or voluntary
compliance. Must create \textbf{structural incentives}: - Assessment
explicitly rewards documentation quality - Templates make documentation
easier (reduce friction) - Automation extracts documentation into
visible outputs (progress dashboards, automated reports) - Peer
recognition for well-documented projects

\textbf{Connection to literature}: Extends (\textbf{stodden2018?})'s
finding that reproducibility requires institutional incentive alignment.

\subsection{6.5.3 The Expertise Distribution
Problem}\label{the-expertise-distribution-problem}

\textbf{Finding}: Reproducible research requires diverse expertise
(research methods, software engineering, data management, domain
knowledge) rarely concentrated in single individuals.

\textbf{Challenge}: VCH Lab students are developing business innovation
expertise, not becoming research software engineers. Expecting students
to master Git, Docker, Conda, Quarto, n8n, statistical methods,
qualitative analysis, and domain knowledge within 4-month projects is
unrealistic.

\textbf{Implication}: Infrastructure must \textbf{distribute expertise}:
- Templates and automation embed software engineering best practices -
Training provides ``just enough'' skills for specific tasks - Technical
support handles complex troubleshooting - Documentation captures
institutional knowledge across cohorts

\textbf{Novel contribution}: Applied research in educational contexts
faces expertise distribution challenge not present in research
institutes where teams include specialists. Infrastructure must
compensate through embedded expertise.

\subsection{6.5.4 The Learning Curve as Investment, Not
Cost}\label{the-learning-curve-as-investment-not-cost}

\textbf{Finding}: Initial learning curve for reproducible research tools
is steep, but returns compound over time.

\textbf{Pilot project evidence}: - Weeks 1-2: High frustration, low
productivity, questioned value - Weeks 3-6: Increasing fluency,
occasional ``wow'' moments when versioning saved work - Weeks 7-12:
Natural workflow, cannot imagine working without version control

Student reflection: ``At first I hated Git, but by the end I felt naked
without it.''

\textbf{Implication}: Must communicate learning curve honestly and frame
as \textbf{investment in professional skills}: - Version control,
computational notebooks, documentation are marketable skills - Portfolio
of reproducible projects demonstrates research quality to employers -
Skills transfer to industry data science roles

\textbf{Reframe}: ``This takes extra time'' → ``This builds
career-relevant capabilities''

\subsection{6.5.5 The Difference Between Reproducibility and
Replicability}\label{the-difference-between-reproducibility-and-replicability}

\textbf{Clarification}: This research focused on
\textbf{reproducibility} (regenerating same results from same data/code)
rather than \textbf{replicability} (conducting new study to validate
findings).

\textbf{Why this matters}: - Reproducibility is \emph{necessary} but
\emph{not sufficient} for scientific validity - Flawed analysis that is
perfectly reproducible remains flawed - Reproducibility enables
verification, which enables trust, which enables building upon findings

\textbf{Implication}: Reproducibility infrastructure must be
complemented by methodological rigor training. Git version control does
not substitute for sound research design.

\textbf{Connection to literature}: Aligns with (\textbf{goodman2016?})'s
distinction between methods reproducibility (this thesis focus) and
results reproducibility (requires replication studies).

\section{6.6 Theoretical Contributions}\label{theoretical-contributions}

This research contributes to reproducibility theory in several ways:

\subsection{6.6.1 Multi-Level Barrier
Framework}\label{multi-level-barrier-framework}

The research proposes a \textbf{multi-level framework} for understanding
reproducibility barriers:

\textbf{Level 1: Individual (skills and motivation)} - Technical skills
(Git, Jupyter, statistical methods) - Methodological training (research
design, documentation) - Intrinsic motivation (curiosity, scientific
values)

\textbf{Level 2: Project (resources and process)} - Time available for
documentation - Tool access and integration - Team collaboration
dynamics - Quality assurance checkpoints

\textbf{Level 3: Organizational (culture and incentives)} - Pedagogical
philosophy - Assessment criteria - Resource allocation - Leadership
commitment - Community norms

\textbf{Level 4: Institutional (infrastructure and policy)} - Technical
infrastructure provision - Data governance policies - Training programs
- Long-term preservation systems - Inter-project knowledge transfer

\textbf{Level 5: Field (disciplinary norms and standards)} -
Methodological traditions - Publication expectations - Funding
requirements - Professional socialization

\textbf{Key insight}: Reproducibility failures typically involve
misalignment across multiple levels. Single-level interventions (e.g.,
providing Git repository without training, or training without
assessment alignment) often fail.

\textbf{Theoretical contribution}: Extends (\textbf{munafo2017?})'s
reproducibility framework by explicitly modeling level interactions and
identifying minimum viable combinations for success.

\subsection{6.6.3 Infrastructure-as-Pedagogy
Concept}\label{infrastructure-as-pedagogy-concept}

The research demonstrates that \textbf{infrastructure can teach} through
embedded design:

\textbf{Traditional view}: Infrastructure (tools, servers) is neutral
platform; pedagogy happens separately through instruction

\textbf{Infrastructure-as-pedagogy view}: Design choices in
infrastructure teach practices: - Git repository template with organized
directory structure teaches file organization - Metadata templates with
required fields teach documentation standards - Pre-commit hooks that
check for large files teach data management - Quarto templates
integrating code and narrative teach literate programming - n8n
automation that validates documentation teaches quality assurance

\textbf{Key insight}: Students learn reproducibility practices by
\emph{using} well-designed infrastructure, not just by \emph{hearing
about} best practices.

\textbf{Theoretical contribution}: Extends ``teaching in the
environment'' pedagogical theory (\textbf{kearney2009?}) into
reproducible research domain. Infrastructure design becomes curriculum
design.

\subsection{6.6.4 Conditional Reproducibility
Concept}\label{conditional-reproducibility-concept}

The research introduces \textbf{conditional reproducibility}:
reproducibility is not binary property but depends on context and
resources:

\textbf{Research is reproducible} \emph{if and only if}:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Technical condition}: Computational environment can be
  reconstructed
\item
  \textbf{Methodological condition}: Procedures are documented
  sufficiently
\item
  \textbf{Data condition}: Data are available (or appropriately
  simulated)
\item
  \textbf{Skill condition}: Reproducer possesses necessary expertise
\item
  \textbf{Resource condition}: Reproducer has access to necessary
  infrastructure
\item
  \textbf{Temporal condition}: Reproduction attempted within
  dependencies' support lifetime
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Implications}: - ``Is this research reproducible?'' is
incomplete question - Better question: ``Under what conditions is this
research reproducible?'' - Reproducibility documentation should specify
conditions explicitly

\textbf{Theoretical contribution}: Provides more nuanced reproducibility
assessment framework than binary reproducible/irreproducible
categorization.

\section{7.1 Research Summary}\label{research-summary}

This thesis investigated reproducible research challenges in applied
supply chain sustainability innovation contexts, using the Value Chain
Hackers Lab at Windesheim University as an empirical case. Through
systematic analysis of five completed research tracks and development of
a reproducible research infrastructure prototype, the research addressed
three interconnected questions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  To what extent can computational notebooks enable reproducible
  research in real-world supply chain sustainability projects?
\item
  What are the main barriers to reproducibility in this domain?
\item
  How do confidentiality, tool limitations, and context specificity
  affect reproducibility?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Principal findings}:

\textbf{Finding 1: Reproducibility failures in VCH Lab are systemic, not
individual} All five analyzed research tracks exhibited serious
reproducibility failures, ranging from partial documentation gaps (Cacao
track: 1.5/3.0) to complete data volatility (Textile track: 0.0/3.0).
Failures stemmed from absent institutional structures rather than
student negligence.

\textbf{Finding 2: Computational notebooks are necessary but not
sufficient} Jupyter Notebooks and RStudio provide powerful
reproducibility capabilities, but effectiveness depends on institutional
infrastructure, educational scaffolding, organizational incentives, and
appropriate research contexts. Tools alone do not create
reproducibility.

\textbf{Finding 3: Barriers are multi-level and interactive} Technical
barriers (lacking version control), organizational barriers (misaligned
pedagogy and assessment), and contextual barriers (methodological
diversity, confidentiality needs) interact multiplicatively.
Single-level interventions typically fail; holistic approaches are
required.

\textbf{Finding 4: Graduated transparency resolves confidentiality
tensions} Business-sensitive applied research can maintain
methodological reproducibility even when full data sharing is
inappropriate. Transparent methods, protected data, and explicit
documentation of constraints balance openness and confidentiality.

\textbf{Finding 5: Infrastructure can teach} Well-designed
infrastructure embeds best practices through templates, automation, and
workflow integration. Students learn reproducibility by using
infrastructure, not just through abstract instruction.

\textbf{Finding 6: Reproducibility enhances applied research
credibility} Counter to concerns that reproducibility requirements would
burden applied research, the pilot project demonstrated that
reproducible practices improve stakeholder confidence, enable knowledge
accumulation, and strengthen research impact.

\section{7.2 Conclusions}\label{conclusions}

\subsection{7.2.1 Answering the Central Research
Problem}\label{answering-the-central-research-problem}

The thesis addressed the overarching problem: \textbf{How can applied
innovation labs build reproducible research capacity in supply chain
sustainability contexts?}

\textbf{Answer}: Through \textbf{integrated socio-technical systems}
combining:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Technical infrastructure} (version control, computational
  notebooks, workflow automation)
\item
  \textbf{Organizational policy} (mandatory standards, assessment
  alignment, quality checkpoints)
\item
  \textbf{Educational programs} (training, templates, mentorship)
\item
  \textbf{Cultural change} (leadership commitment, community norms,
  visible benefits)
\item
  \textbf{Contextual adaptation} (graduated transparency, methodological
  flexibility, boundary documentation)
\end{enumerate}

No single component suffices; all five must work together.

\subsection{7.2.2 Theoretical
Significance}\label{theoretical-significance}

This research contributes to reproducibility science by:

\textbf{Extending reproducibility frameworks to applied contexts}: Most
reproducibility literature focuses on academic science. This thesis
demonstrates how reproducibility principles must be adapted (not
abandoned) for business innovation research with confidentiality
constraints, stakeholder complexity, and practitioner audiences.

\textbf{Demonstrating importance of organizational culture}: Technical
capabilities (tools, infrastructure) are necessary but insufficient.
Organizational culture determines whether capabilities are adopted. This
validates and extends organizational change theories into reproducible
research domain.

\textbf{Introducing conditional reproducibility concept}:
Reproducibility is not binary property but depends on multiple
conditions (computational environment, documentation quality, data
availability, reproducer skills, infrastructure access). This framework
enables more nuanced reproducibility assessment.

\textbf{Proposing infrastructure-as-pedagogy}: Thoughtfully designed
infrastructure teaches practices through use. This concept bridges
technology design and educational theory.

\subsection{7.2.3 Practical
Significance}\label{practical-significance-1}

\textbf{For Windesheim and VCH Lab}: The infrastructure provides
immediate pathway to improve research quality, enabling knowledge
accumulation across project cohorts, strengthening credibility with
industry partners, and supporting accreditation through demonstrable
research rigor.

\textbf{For applied research community}: The methodology, infrastructure
design, and lessons learned provide transferable guidance for other
innovation labs, practice-oriented research programs, and educational
research contexts facing similar reproducibility challenges.

\textbf{For sustainability practitioners}: Reproducible research
practices enable verification of sustainability claims, reducing risk of
implementing ineffective interventions and supporting evidence-based
decision-making in supply chain sustainability.

\subsection{7.2.4 Methodological
Significance}\label{methodological-significance}

The research demonstrates value of \textbf{combining retrospective case
study analysis with prospective design science}. Case studies diagnose
problems and identify requirements; design science develops and tests
solutions. This combination generates both theoretical insights and
actionable artifacts.

\textbf{Recommendation 1.1: Mandate reproducibility infrastructure for
all projects}

\emph{Rationale}: Voluntary adoption has failed repeatedly. Mandatory
infrastructure use creates common baseline.

\emph{Implementation}: - All new VCH Lab projects must initialize from
provided Git repository template - Project registration includes
infrastructure setup verification - Supervisors trained to assess
reproducibility in addition to outputs

\textbf{Recommendation 1.2: Align assessment criteria with
reproducibility}

\emph{Rationale}: Students rationally allocate effort to graded
dimensions. Documentation must carry substantial weight.

\emph{Implementation}: - GP1 rubric: Add ``Reproducibility and
Documentation Quality'' as 20\% of grade - GP2 rubric: Require data and
code availability for passing grade - GP3/4/5 rubrics: Include
reproducibility audit in assessment

\textbf{Recommendation 1.3: Integrate reproducibility training into
onboarding}

\emph{Rationale}: Infrastructure without training creates frustration
and abandonment.

\emph{Implementation}: - Develop 2-hour workshop covering Git basics,
Jupyter workflow, template usage - Create video tutorials for
asynchronous learning - Assign peer mentors (prior cohort students) for
troubleshooting - Embed training in first project week, not optional
pre-work

\textbf{Recommendation 1.4: Appoint reproducibility coordinator role}

\emph{Rationale}: Infrastructure requires ongoing maintenance,
troubleshooting, and improvement.

\emph{Implementation}: - Assign 0.2 FTE staff position for
reproducibility coordination - Responsibilities: Technical support,
template updates, training delivery, quality monitoring - Collaborate
with IT support for server administration

\textbf{Recommendation 1.5: Create reproducibility showcase}

\emph{Rationale}: Visible examples motivate adoption and provide
learning resources.

\emph{Implementation}: - Publish 3-5 exemplar projects publicly on VCH
Lab website - Feature student interviews describing benefits - Use in
marketing to prospective students and industry partners - Update
annually with best projects

\section{7.6 Final Reflections}\label{final-reflections}

Reproducibility is not just a technical challenge---it is fundamentally
about \textbf{trust, cumulative knowledge, and scientific
responsibility}.

In academic science, reproducibility enables verification and building
upon prior work. In applied business innovation research,
reproducibility serves additional functions:

\textbf{Trust}: Industry partners investing in research deserve
confidence that findings are reliable \textbf{Learning}: Educational
institutions must enable knowledge to accumulate across student cohorts
\textbf{Impact}: Sustainability interventions should be based on
verifiable evidence, not hopeful claims \textbf{Accountability}: Public
and private investments in applied research require demonstrable rigor

The reproducibility crisis threatens all these functions. But crisis
also creates opportunity for transformation.

This thesis demonstrates that reproducibility in applied contexts is
\textbf{achievable}. It requires investment, commitment, and systemic
change---but it is neither impossible nor impractical. The pilot project
succeeded. Students, initially resistant, became advocates. Industry
partners recognized value. Independent verification confirmed
reproducibility.

Most importantly: \textbf{reproducibility enhances rather than
constrains creativity}. Clear documentation enables bolder
experimentation (knowing work can be recreated if it fails). Version
control enables exploration without fear of losing prior work.
Transparent methods invite collaboration and improvement.

The path forward requires collective action: - \textbf{Institutions}
must invest in infrastructure and align incentives - \textbf{Educators}
must teach reproducibility as core competency - \textbf{Students} must
embrace documentation as craft, not burden - \textbf{Industry partners}
must value process transparency alongside outcomes - \textbf{Funders}
must support reproducibility infrastructure - \textbf{Professional
communities} must establish and enforce standards

Windesheim's VCH Lab has the opportunity to lead. The infrastructure
exists. The pilot succeeded. The recommendations are actionable.

The question is not whether reproducible applied research is possible,
but whether we have the commitment to make it standard practice.

This thesis provides the evidence, the tools, and the roadmap. The next
chapter belongs to VCH Lab, to Windesheim, and to the broader community
of applied innovation researchers.

\textbf{Let us build research systems worthy of the sustainability
challenges we seek to address.}

\section{8.1 Personal Learning Journey}\label{personal-learning-journey}

This graduation project has been transformative in developing my
capabilities as an innovation professional. Reflecting on the BI
Meta-skills framework, I recognize substantial growth across all five
domains:

\subsection{8.1.1 DEFINE: Uncovering the Right
Problem}\label{define-uncovering-the-right-problem}

\textbf{What I learned:} At the start, I viewed reproducibility as
primarily a technical problem---``students need Git training.'' Through
systematic case study analysis, I discovered the problem was far deeper:
systemic organizational, cultural, and incentive misalignment.

The process of \textbf{properly defining} the reproducibility challenge
required: - Resisting premature solution-jumping - Investigating root
causes, not just symptoms - Considering multiple perspectives (students,
supervisors, partners) - Recognizing that the ``problem'' was
multi-layered

\textbf{How I grew:} I became more comfortable with ambiguity and
complexity. Early in the project, I wanted clear, simple answers. By
mid-project, I understood that valuable problems are rarely simple. The
capacity to sit with complexity without forcing premature clarity is a
meta-skill I will carry forward.

\textbf{Evidence:} - Case study analysis evolved through three
iterations as understanding deepened - Research questions were refined
twice based on emerging insights - Final problem definition (Chapter
1.4) is substantially more sophisticated than initial conception

\subsection{8.1.2 DESIGN: Developing
Solutions}\label{design-developing-solutions}

\textbf{What I learned:} Solution design requires balancing multiple
competing constraints: - Technical feasibility vs.~user accessibility -
Comprehensive functionality vs.~manageable complexity - Ideal practices
vs.~practical realities - Standardization vs.~flexibility

The iterative design process taught me that \textbf{first solutions are
rarely optimal solutions}. The infrastructure went through four major
iterations based on pilot feedback.

\textbf{How I grew:} I developed capacity for \textbf{design thinking
under constraints}---not just imagining ideal solutions, but designing
within real limitations (student expertise, supervisor time, server
resources, organizational culture).

I also learned to design \textbf{for adoption}, not just for
functionality. The infrastructure needed to work technically \emph{and}
be acceptable to users---these are distinct challenges requiring
different design thinking.

\textbf{Evidence:} - Infrastructure architecture diagram (Chapter 5.2.2)
reflects integration of multiple requirements - Template iterations
based on user feedback - Graduated transparency model balances openness
and confidentiality

\subsection{8.1.3 EXECUTE: Implementation and
Delivery}\label{execute-implementation-and-delivery}

\textbf{What I learned:} Execution requires different skills than
design. Technical implementation, stakeholder management, timeline
management, quality assurance---each demands distinct competencies.

I learned the importance of \textbf{incremental delivery}. Rather than
waiting for ``perfect'' infrastructure, I deployed minimum viable
versions, gathered feedback, and iterated. This de-risked the project
and kept stakeholders engaged.

\textbf{How I grew:} I developed \textbf{project management discipline}:
maintaining task lists, tracking progress, managing dependencies,
communicating transparently about challenges.

I also grew in \textbf{resilience}. Multiple setbacks occurred (pilot
students initially resistant, reproducibility audit found bugs, timeline
pressure intense). Maintaining momentum despite obstacles required
emotional regulation and problem-solving persistence.

\textbf{Evidence:} - Pilot project successfully completed within 12-week
timeline - Reproducibility audit demonstrated working infrastructure -
Documentation completeness enabling future users - This thesis delivered
on time despite substantial scope

\subsection{8.1.4 LEARN: Monitoring and
Adapting}\label{learn-monitoring-and-adapting}

\textbf{What I learned:} Research is inherently iterative. Every finding
raises new questions. Every implementation reveals new challenges. The
capacity to \textbf{learn from experience} and adapt accordingly is
perhaps the most valuable meta-skill.

I learned to \textbf{embrace failure as information}. When templates
proved too complex, that wasn't ``failure''---it was valuable feedback
guiding simpler designs. When students struggled with Git, that wasn't
``incompetence''---it was signal that training needed improvement.

\textbf{How I grew:} I developed \textbf{reflective practice}:
deliberately pausing to ask ``what am I learning?'' and ``what should I
do differently?'' This reflection discipline transformed experiences
into growth.

I also developed \textbf{comfort with uncertainty}. Research doesn't
provide clean answers. Many questions remain open. Being okay with ``I
don't fully know yet'' is maturity.

\textbf{Evidence:} - Multiple infrastructure iterations based on
learnings - Explicit documentation of limitations and future directions
(Chapter 7.4) - This reflection chapter demonstrating metacognitive
awareness

\subsection{8.1.5 LEAD: Managing Process and
Stakeholders}\label{lead-managing-process-and-stakeholders}

\textbf{What I learned:} Leadership in research context means: -
\textbf{Managing expectations}: Communicating realistic timelines and
trade-offs - \textbf{Building buy-in}: Helping stakeholders understand
value and address concerns - \textbf{Facilitating collaboration}:
Coordinating across students, supervisors, IT support -
\textbf{Championing vision}: Maintaining focus on reproducibility
mission amid competing demands

\textbf{How I grew:} I learned to \textbf{lead through influence} rather
than authority. I had no formal power over VCH Lab students or
coordinators, yet needed their cooperation. This required persuasion,
relationship-building, and demonstrating value.

I also developed \textbf{stakeholder empathy}. Initial frustration when
students resisted infrastructure transformed into understanding their
perspective: they're learning business innovation, not becoming software
engineers. This empathy led to better design choices.

\textbf{Evidence:} - Successful pilot project coordination across
multiple stakeholders - Positive feedback from pilot participants
despite initial resistance - Infrastructure adoption by VCH Lab
coordinator for future projects - Recommendations (Chapter 7.3)
demonstrating strategic thinking

\section{8.4 Challenges and How They Were
Overcome}\label{challenges-and-how-they-were-overcome}

\subsection{Challenge 1: Scope
Management}\label{challenge-1-scope-management}

\textbf{Problem}: Initial project scope was enormous---could have
written thesis on any of: reproducibility theory, infrastructure design,
case study analysis, qualitative methods, stakeholder perspectives,
economic analysis.

\textbf{Solution}: Ruthlessly prioritized. Focused on computational
reproducibility in VCH Lab context. Explicitly scoped out qualitative
methods and economic analysis as future work.

\textbf{Learning}: Completeness is impossible; strategic incompleteness
is necessary. Better to do focused work deeply than superficial work
broadly.

\subsection{Challenge 2: Technical
Debugging}\label{challenge-2-technical-debugging}

\textbf{Problem}: Multiple infrastructure components (JupyterHub, Git,
Quarto, n8n) with complex interactions. When things broke, diagnosis was
difficult.

\textbf{Solution}: Systematic debugging methodology: isolate components,
test individually, reintegrate incrementally. Maintained detailed logs
of configuration changes. Built test cases for reproducibility.

\textbf{Learning}: Complex systems require disciplined debugging
practices. Intuition and trial-and-error are insufficient. Systematic
approaches save time.

\subsection{Challenge 3: Stakeholder
Resistance}\label{challenge-3-stakeholder-resistance}

\textbf{Problem}: Initial pilot student reactions included skepticism:
``This seems like extra work,'' ``Why can't we just use Google Drive?''

\textbf{Solution}: Empathy and demonstration. Acknowledged learning
curve honestly. Showed early benefits (recovered deleted work via Git,
generated report from notebooks automatically). Positioned
reproducibility as career-relevant skill, not academic obligation.

\textbf{Learning}: Change resistance is legitimate, not obstinate. Meet
people where they are. Benefits must be visible early to sustain
engagement.

\subsection{Challenge 4: Time Pressure}\label{challenge-4-time-pressure}

\textbf{Problem}: Balancing deep research (requires time) with
graduation deadline (fixed) created constant tension.

\textbf{Solution}: Aggressive timeline with milestone tracking.
Prioritized ``good enough'' over ``perfect'' in early stages, with plan
to iterate. Accepted that some questions would remain open.

\textbf{Learning}: Perfect is enemy of done. Strategic satisficing
(sufficient + satisfying) is valuable skill. Knowing what to defer
matters as much as knowing what to do.

\subsection{Challenge 5: Researcher
Bias}\label{challenge-5-researcher-bias}

\textbf{Problem}: As infrastructure developer, I had vested interest in
``proving'' it worked, creating potential confirmation bias.

\textbf{Solution}: Engaged independent reproducibility auditor. Actively
sought disconfirming evidence in case studies. Documented limitations
explicitly. Triangulated findings across data sources.

\textbf{Learning}: Reflexivity (examining own biases) is essential
research practice. Transparency about positionality and limitations
enhances rather than diminishes credibility.

\section{8.5 Ethical Reflections}\label{ethical-reflections}

Several ethical dimensions merit reflection:

\subsection{8.5.1 Student Work Analysis}\label{student-work-analysis}

Analyzing prior students' work (case studies) raised ethical questions:
Am I being fair? Am I respecting their efforts? Am I causing potential
embarrassment?

\textbf{Resolution}: - Anonymized student identities - Framed failures
as systemic, not individual (``absent infrastructure'' not ``lazy
students'') - Acknowledged students operated within constraints not of
their making - Emphasized learning purpose, not judgment

\textbf{Reflection}: Critique and respect can coexist. Systems critique
is different from personal criticism.

\subsection{8.5.2 Reproducibility
Requirements}\label{reproducibility-requirements}

Imposing reproducibility requirements on students raises fairness
questions: Is this adding burden? Are we changing expectations
mid-program?

\textbf{Resolution}: - Phased implementation (new cohorts, not
mid-project changes) - Provided substantial support (training,
templates, troubleshooting) - Positioned as skill development, not
compliance - Ensured assessment alignment so effort rewarded

\textbf{Reflection}: New standards must be introduced equitably with
adequate support.

\subsection{8.5.3 Open Science and
Confidentiality}\label{open-science-and-confidentiality}

Tension between open science ideals (full transparency) and business
realities (confidentiality) required ethical navigation.

\textbf{Resolution}: - Graduated transparency model respecting
legitimate confidentiality - Prioritized methods transparency even when
data must be protected - Explicit documentation of constraints -
Rejected false dichotomy (either fully open or not reproducible)

\textbf{Reflection}: Ethical research means navigating competing values
thoughtfully, not applying absolute principles rigidly.

\section{8.6 What I Would Do
Differently}\label{what-i-would-do-differently}

With hindsight, several aspects could have been improved:

\textbf{Start infrastructure development earlier}: I conducted case
studies first, then built infrastructure. Parallel development would
have saved time and enabled earlier pilot testing.

\textbf{Engage more users in pilot}: Single pilot project (3 students)
provided valuable feedback but limited sample size. Multiple concurrent
pilots would have accelerated learning.

\textbf{Record detailed decision rationale throughout}: While I
documented decisions, deeper real-time rationale capture would have
enriched thesis discussion. I reconstructed some reasoning
retrospectively.

\textbf{Involve industry partners more directly}: Limited direct
engagement with business stakeholders. Their perspectives on
reproducibility value would have strengthened the research.

\textbf{Develop qualitative methods further}: Computational focus left
qualitative reproducibility underdeveloped. Allocating more time here
would have produced more complete solution.

\section{8.7 Advice for Future Graduation
Students}\label{advice-for-future-graduation-students}

Based on this experience, I would advise future students:

\textbf{Define before designing}: Invest time understanding the problem
deeply. Premature solutions are rarely optimal. The temptation to
``start building'' is strong---resist it initially.

\textbf{Embrace iteration}: First drafts are terrible. First prototypes
break. First research questions are often wrong. That's normal. Build
iteration into timeline.

\textbf{Seek feedback early and often}: Don't wait until work is
``ready'' to show others. Rough drafts generate better feedback than
polished but misdirected work.

\textbf{Manage your energy}: Research marathons, not sprints.
Sustainable pace matters more than heroic bursts followed by burnout.
Schedule rest.

\textbf{Document as you go}: Future-you will forget today's reasoning.
Write it down now. Research logs, decision notes, reflection
journals---all valuable.

\textbf{Connect with your ``why''}: When motivation wanes (it will),
reconnect with why the work matters. My ``why'' was enabling better
sustainability research---that sustained me through difficult moments.

\textbf{Balance depth and breadth}: You cannot research everything.
Focus depth where it matters most. Acknowledge but don't fully explore
every tangent.

\textbf{Enjoy the process}: Graduation project is rare opportunity for
deep, self-directed investigation. Despite stress, I genuinely enjoyed
the intellectual challenge. Try to savor it.

humanity faces.

\section{Appendix C: Interview Protocol
Example}\label{appendix-c-interview-protocol-example}

\textbf{Semi-Structured Interview Guide: VCH Lab Coordinator}

\emph{Estimated duration: 60-90 minutes}

\textbf{Opening (5 minutes)} - Introduce research purpose and process -
Confirm consent and recording permission - Explain how data will be used
- Invite questions

\textbf{Section 1: Current VCH Lab Research Practices (20 minutes)}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  Can you describe how a typical VCH Lab project progresses from
  initiation to completion?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: What are the key milestones?
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Who is involved at each stage?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What guidance do students receive about research methodology and
  documentation?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Are there required templates or standards?
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: How is quality assessed?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What tools and platforms do students typically use?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Are certain tools required vs.~optional?
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: What technical support is available?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  How are completed projects archived and made accessible for future
  reference?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Section 2: Reproducibility Challenges (25 minutes)}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{4}
\tightlist
\item
  In your experience, what challenges do students face in documenting
  their research process?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Time constraints?
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Technical skills?
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Motivation/incentives?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  Can you share examples of past projects where documentation was
  particularly strong or weak?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: What made the difference?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  How do industry partners react to the research outputs from VCH Lab?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Do they ever question methodology or request more
    transparency?
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What happens when a new project wants to build on prior work?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: How easy is it to access and understand previous
    projects?
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Section 3: Infrastructure and Support (15 minutes)}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{8}
\tightlist
\item
  What technical infrastructure is currently available to support
  reproducible research?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Compute resources, storage, software
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What barriers exist to implementing better reproducibility practices?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Technical, organizational, cultural, resource
  \end{itemize}
\item
  What support would be most valuable for improving research quality?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Section 4: Future Vision (15 minutes)}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\setcounter{enumi}{11}
\item
  If you could design the ideal reproducible research system for VCH
  Lab, what would it look like?
\item
  What concerns do you have about implementing more structured
  reproducibility requirements?

  \begin{itemize}
  \tightlist
  \item
    \emph{Probe}: Student resistance, supervisor workload, industry
    partner expectations
  \end{itemize}
\item
  How would you measure success of reproducibility infrastructure?
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Closing (5 minutes)} - Invite any additional thoughts or
questions - Explain next steps in the research - Offer to share findings
- Thank participant

\textbf{Post-Interview Notes Template} - Setting and atmosphere -
Non-verbal observations - Topics that generated strong reactions -
Potential follow-up questions - Initial reflections
